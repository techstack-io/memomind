## Memo Interpretation

Most of us already know how to send someone good wishes. We do it easily for people we love — hoping things go well for them, wanting them spared pain, holding them warmly in mind even from a distance. That's a real capacity, and it matters. But it also has an edge, usually one we haven't looked at closely: it tends to stop right around the people we already like.

This slogan asks for something more deliberate. On the in-breath, you take in what's usually pushed away — someone else's pain, or your own discomfort, the thing you'd normally avoid looking at directly. On the out-breath, you send out what's usually held onto tightly — ease, relief, well-being. It's a full reversal of the ordinary habit: grasping what feels good, pushing off what doesn't. Practiced this way, on purpose, it starts to loosen a grip most people don't even notice they're holding.

The harder half of this isn't the version aimed at people you already love — that part can feel almost natural. The harder half is turning it toward someone specific you genuinely dislike, disagree with, or find difficult to be around. Not an abstract enemy, not humanity in general — someone real, someone you know, someone your compassion currently has a clear boundary around. That's where the practice stops being a nice idea and starts actually asking something of you. It can be uncomfortable to see just how much energy goes into protecting your own comfort, your own preferences, your own sense of who deserves your care and who doesn't.

None of this works from strain. This practice draws its ground directly from the resting you'd return to in stillness — you gather the willingness for it from that same open place, and when a breath or a session of this gets hard, you're allowed to return there rather than push through on effort alone. And at the end of each breath, whatever you've taken in or sent out, you let it go. No need to hold on to it, analyze it, or carry it forward. Light touch, breath by breath.

## Conversation Guidance

This slogan tends to land with someone who notices a real asymmetry in their own compassion — generous and available for some people, quietly absent for others — or who's aware of a habitual pattern of avoiding their own discomfort while chasing whatever feels good.

**Trap:** treating the existing capacity for warmth toward people already loved as evidence that compassion is fully developed — mistaking the easy, comfort-zone version of care for the whole of it.

**Shift:** naming, gently, where the edge of that compassion actually sits — not to induce guilt, but to notice it as information. Compassion with a clear boundary around who qualifies is still compassion, just not yet the full practice this slogan points toward.

**Practice:** something specific and slightly uncomfortable — not the person someone already loves easily, and not their worst enemy either, but someone real, known personally, mildly or moderately disliked. The invitation is to imagine, even briefly, breathing in some of what that person is actually carrying, and breathing out something easier for them. Keep it light — one breath, then let it go, rather than dwelling on it.

Avoid pushing this toward someone who has caused the person real harm, or toward self-directed tonglen without care — this slogan's edge is meant to expand compassion, not to demand it indiscriminately or turn it into another way of overriding one's own needs.

## Safety

Should not be used to encourage someone to extend care toward a person who is actively harming them, or to reinforce a pattern of absorbing mistreatment from someone exploiting their compassion. Tonglen concerns the willingness to feel discomfort on behalf of others by choice, in a contained practice — not tolerating ongoing harm, staying in an unsafe relationship, or over-functioning as caretaker for someone who consistently depletes them.

Be attentive to signs of compassion fatigue, caregiver burnout, or a pattern of self-neglect in favor of others' needs. For someone already stretched thin from over-giving, the instruction to "take in what you push away" could reinforce an existing harmful pattern rather than open a new capacity. In that case, the more appropriate move is toward this slogan's own connection to self-directed care (see Slogan 10), or away from this reflection entirely toward rest and boundary-setting.
