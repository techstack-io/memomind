## Memo Interpretation

Before anything else in this practice, there's a first move that has to happen — and it isn't a technique. It's a decision about how you're going to look at your own life.

Think of it like putting on a pair of polarized sunglasses before walking out into blinding light. The sunglasses don't change the sun, the road, or the traffic. What they change is your ability to see clearly through the glare. Without them, the glare is all you can perceive — reflex, reaction, squinting against whatever's too bright to look at directly. With them on, the same world is suddenly navigable. Nothing out there changed. What changed is what was standing between you and seeing it plainly.

The glasses are easy to put on for a moment and just as easy to take back off the second things get uncomfortable — the second acceptance feels too hard, or responsibility feels like too much to carry. What keeps them on isn't willpower. It's remembering what's actually true. Traditionally, four things are held in mind. These are what are called The Four Reminders:

- The rarity and preciousness of human life.
- The certainty of change and, eventually, death — impermanence.
- The weight of our own actions, and how they shape what comes next — karma.
- The quiet suffering of living on autopilot, reacting instead of seeing.

Take these seriously enough, and long enough, and they change how you see everything else. They're worth sitting with more fully in the library — for now, know that together, they're what stops you from setting the glasses back down.

The preliminaries are that first shift — before Tonglen, before working with difficult people, before any of the slogans that come later, you have to be willing to take off the glare and look. And the glare, for most of us, is made of two things: old pain we haven't actually looked at, and the reflex to make sense of it through blame or self-pity instead of ownership.

This starts with acceptance — not resignation, and not agreement that what happened was fine. Acceptance here means being willing to see your life exactly as it is, without the distortion of "this shouldn't have happened" or "if only things had gone differently." Whatever you're carrying — the divorce, the loss, the years you spent somewhere you didn't want to be — happened. Accepting that isn't giving up. It's putting the glasses on.

What follows acceptance is responsibility. Not blame — responsibility. Even when something wasn't your fault, and even when there's a very real party who could be blamed, holding onto that blame keeps you exactly where the pain first put you. Somewhere in that holding, self-pity creeps in too — the sense that your suffering is uniquely unfair, that things happening the way they did means something's owed to you. Both of these feel understandable. Neither one moves you forward.

Training in the preliminaries means recognizing something simpler and, honestly, sturdier: whatever has happened, you are the one who has to live the life that comes next. No one else is equipped to do that for you. Not because no one cares, but because it's yours — your history, your body, your one life moving forward from here. Once that's actually accepted, something shifts. You stop waiting for the injustice to be corrected before you're allowed to move. You start acting from where you actually are.

This is not the same as saying you have to face it alone. Taking responsibility for your life doesn't mean refusing help, support, or the people who show up for you—it means no one else can do the inner work of meeting what's happened with honesty instead of blame. That part is yours. Leaning on others while you do it is not a failure of this practice; it's often exactly what makes it possible.

This is why it comes first, before anything else in the practice. Every slogan that follows—working with difficult emotions, holding compassion for people who've hurt you, staying steady when things go wrong—depends on this first move already being made. You can't do the rest of the work through glare. Take the glasses off first. See clearly. Then begin.

## Conversation Guidance

The first time this slogan comes up for someone—likely during onboarding, but possibly the first time their situation calls for it—Ana can offer the sunglasses image directly. It's a memorable way of explaining the difference between reacting automatically and seeing clearly.

After that, Ana should not repeatedly mention the sunglasses metaphor. Instead, she should embody the underlying movement of the teaching in fresh language: acceptance before responsibility, clarity before reaction, agency without self-blame.

This slogan commonly applies when someone:

- feels stuck or overwhelmed by life
- is trapped in self-pity or resentment
- keeps waiting for circumstances to change before moving forward
- feels powerless or discouraged
- catastrophizes about the future

Avoid rushing into the teaching when someone is in fresh grief, active trauma, or immediate crisis. In those moments, acknowledgment and compassionate presence come before philosophical reflection.

## Safety

This slogan can unintentionally reinforce trauma-driven hyper-independence if applied carelessly.

If someone already believes they must carry everything alone, avoid language suggesting they are solely responsible for coping without support. The teaching concerns ownership of one's inner response, not isolation from others.

Likewise, never use this slogan to imply someone is responsible for abuse, neglect, or harm inflicted upon them. Responsibility begins with how we meet reality now—not with accepting blame for what another person chose to do.

If ongoing abuse, immediate danger, severe trauma, or acute crisis is present, prioritize safety, stabilization, and appropriate support rather than delivering this teaching.
