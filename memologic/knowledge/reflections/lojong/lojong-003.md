## Memo Interpretation

The previous practice asked you to notice how solid the world outside you seems — how total and permanent things can feel while you're caught up in them. This one turns the same question inward. Not "how solid does the world seem," but "how solid does the one looking at the world seem?"

Try it directly, the way this is actually meant to be practiced, not just thought about. Somewhere in your experience right now, there's a sense of "me" — the one reading this, the one who will have a reaction to it in a second. Go look for that one. Not your thoughts about yourself, not your name or your history — the actual "you" all of that is supposed to belong to. Where does it live? Where did it come from? Where does it go when you're not thinking about it?

This isn't a single question you answer once. It's a practice you return to, over and over: notice a thought or a perception arising, notice how solid the "one having the thought" and the "thing being perceived" both seem — the perceiver here, the perceived there, this and that. Then look before that division even starts. What's actually there, underneath "this" and "that," before the mind sorts everything into inside and outside?

Most people, looking honestly and repeatedly, come back without a clean answer. You'll find thoughts. A mood. A sensation. What's much harder to find is a fixed someone standing behind all of it, unchanging, in a specific location. And every time you think you've caught it — "there, that's me, right there" — the catching itself becomes the next thought, and the ordinary sense of self quietly reassembles around it.

This is why the traditional phrase for this slogan calls awareness "unborn." Not because nothing is happening — awareness is unmistakably, vividly present — but because it has no discoverable birthplace and no discoverable destination. It doesn't come from somewhere and go somewhere else. It's simply here, the way the sky is here, without needing an origin story to be real.

None of this is meant to be unsettling for its own sake, though it often is at first — this practice is traditionally understood as more disorienting than simply noticing that experience is dreamlike, because now you're looking at the one who was doing the noticing. That disorientation isn't a sign something's wrong. It's what happens when a very old, very automatic assumption gets looked at directly for the first time.

What tends to loosen, with this kind of repeated looking, is the felt need to defend a fixed idea of who you are. If the "you" at the center of everything was never quite as solid or findable as it seemed, there's less, in a strange way, to protect. Not because you've lost something real, but because what you were defending so carefully turns out to have been more like a habit of thought than a location you could ever actually be pinned down to.

## Conversation Guidance

This slogan applies when someone is genuinely curious or reflective about identity — questioning who they are beneath their roles, thoughts, or self-image, in a way that feels exploratory rather than distressing.

There are two levels of practice here, and Ana should generally start with the gentler one.

**First offering — the observing self.** Thoughts, emotions, and memories continually arise and pass. What stays constant is the awareness noticing them. This can be practiced simply:

1. Spend a few minutes simply observing your breathing.
2. When a thought or feeling arises, gently notice it — you don't need to follow it or push it away.
3. Return your attention to the breath.
4. Notice: the thoughts keep changing. The noticing itself doesn't seem to change the same way.

This version is stabilizing, not destabilizing — it gives the person a steady place to stand (the awareness itself) rather than pulling that ground away. It's appropriate for most people curious about this territory.

**Second offering — looking for the looker.** Only for someone who seems genuinely ready to go further, ideally after some familiarity with the first practice. This is a more direct, more unsettling inquiry:

1. Notice a thought, feeling, or something you're perceiving right now — anything simple.
2. Notice how solid and separate the "you" noticing it, and the thing being noticed, seem. There's a perceiver here, and something perceived there.
3. Now look for the awareness itself — before it splits into a "this" and a "that." What's there, underneath the noticing, before the mind sorts it into inside and outside?
4. Don't try to answer with a concept. Just look. If nothing definite is found, that's not a failure — that's the point of the practice.

Ana should offer either practice slowly, without insisting on a particular outcome, without telling the person what they should find, and without following up right away with pressure to report back an answer. The second offering in particular is closer to an invitation than an exercise with a correct result, and should never be pushed on someone who hasn't shown readiness for it.

This slogan commonly applies when someone:

- is reflecting on identity in a curious, open way
- describes feeling less solid or certain about who they are, without distress
- is examining a belief like "this is just who I am" and wondering if that's actually true
- describes a spontaneous quiet or spacious moment where the usual sense of self briefly loosened
- explicitly asks for something to try or practice, not just something to think about

Avoid this slogan entirely if someone describes symptoms resembling depersonalization or derealization, dissociation, or distress about not feeling like themselves. In those cases, even the gentler observing-self practice should be approached with caution, and the deeper inquiry should not be offered at all.

## Safety

This slogan requires more caution than most, and traditional teachers in this lineage explicitly describe the deeper inquiry as more unsettling than the slogan before it. Examining the nature of self can be genuinely destabilizing for someone whose sense of self is already fragile, whether from a mental health condition, a dissociative episode, trauma, or acute distress.

The two-tier structure exists specifically to manage this risk: the observing-self practice is gentle and stabilizing, appropriate for most people curious about this territory. The deeper "looking for the looker" inquiry is not — it should only be offered to someone who has shown real readiness, ideally with some familiarity with the gentler practice first, and never to someone showing any sign of fragility around their sense of self.

Never introduce either practice, but especially the deeper one, to someone describing a sense of unreality, disconnection from themselves, or a feeling that nothing feels solid or true — language consistent with depersonalization or derealization. For these individuals, exploring the absence of a fixed self is not liberating — it can intensify an already distressing loss of grounding. If there is any indication of this, do not proceed with this slogan; instead, prioritize grounding, presence, and encouraging professional support.

This slogan is intended for someone with a stable, secure baseline sense of self who is curious to examine it directly — not for someone already struggling to feel like a coherent, continuous person. When in doubt, do not apply this teaching, or offer only the gentle observing-self version.
