## Memo Interpretation

Most of us are sorting people constantly, usually without noticing we're doing it. Someone becomes a friend, an irritant, or simply invisible, often within seconds of meeting them — and once that sorting happens, it tends to stay put. The friend can do very little wrong. The irritant can do very little right. And the invisible ones stop registering as people worth attention at all.

None of that sorting is the actual problem. Recognizing that someone is trustworthy, difficult, or simply not relevant to you right now is just ordinary perception — sometimes a useful one. The problem starts when the label stops describing a current situation and starts pretending to describe a permanent fact. The friend isn't just someone you get along with right now — they become someone incapable of being wrong. The difficult person isn't just someone grating on you today — they become, in your mind, simply made that way, fixed, beyond your control. The neutral ones aren't just outside your attention right now — they become people who don't fully count.

Each of these frozen labels comes with its own habitual reaction. Toward what's loved: grasping, wanting to hold on, unable to just enjoy without needing to possess. Toward what's disliked: real hostility, not just discomfort but something closer to wanting the other person to pay for how they've made you feel. Toward what's neutral: a kind of active not-caring, a decision — usually unexamined — that certain people simply don't warrant your notice.

The real move this slogan asks for isn't to stop having reactions. It's to catch them earlier, before affection hardens into grasping, before irritation hardens into hostility, before neutrality hardens into indifference. And when a reaction does show up in full force, the practice is to own it as something you're generating, not something the other person is doing to you. Without another person to blame it on, the reaction has nowhere left to stand — and that's usually where it starts to soften.

## Conversation Guidance

This slogan tends to land with someone describing a relationship — or a whole category of people — they've stopped examining: a person they've written off completely, a favorite they can't see clearly, or a group they've noticed they simply don't think about.

**Trap:** treating the reaction as simply accurate information about the other person — "they're just like that," "I don't need to think about them" — rather than recognizing a label that's calcified past the point of actually looking.

**Shift:** naming, without judgment, that the intensity of the reaction belongs to the person having it as much as to whoever triggered it. This isn't about deciding the reaction is wrong or unjustified — it's about noticing where it's being generated.

**Practice:** a specific self-inventory, drawn straight from the slogan's own structure — who am I gripping too tightly right now? Where am I pushing back harder than the situation calls for? Who have I stopped bothering to notice at all? Picking just one of the three and looking at it honestly, without needing to fix it immediately, is usually enough for one turn.

Avoid turning this into a demand for the person to like someone they have real reasons to be cautious around, or to force themselves to feel connected to everyone equally. The slogan concerns the *habitual hardening* of a reaction, not the reaction's underlying legitimacy.

## Safety

Should not be used to suggest that someone dismiss real, well-founded caution or dislike toward a person who has caused them harm. This slogan concerns the *unexamined hardening* of ordinary reactions into fixed, unquestioned categories — not a demand to soften every boundary or extend goodwill to someone who has demonstrated they're unsafe.

Be especially careful when "the person I can't stand" in someone's message turns out to be someone actively harming or abusing them. In that case, this is not the right reflection — the priority is validating the person's read on the situation and supporting appropriate boundaries, not inviting them to examine their own reaction as the source of the problem.
