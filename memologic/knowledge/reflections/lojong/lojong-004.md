## Memo Interpretation

There's a particular trap that only opens up after the last two practices start to work. Once you've spent real time noticing how dreamlike experience is, and how hard it is to find a fixed self underneath your thoughts, something shifts. Life can start to feel a little strange, a little distant, oddly weightless. For some people, this is where a new problem quietly begins, and it tends to show up in one of two ways.

The first is conceptual. If none of this destabilizes you — if instead it feels satisfying, even exciting — you might start to think something like: everything really is empty, there's no fixed "me" in here after all, so I'm free of all my old worries now. This can feel like arrival. Concluding "I've figured it out, I'm free now" isn't actually the fruit of the practice — it's a new trap wearing the practice's clothes. You haven't stopped clinging to something solid. You've just swapped what you're clinging to: instead of a fixed self, you're now clinging to the idea of having transcended one.

The second trap is quieter and easier to miss, because it can look like progress from the outside: using the practice to feel separate from everyone and everything else. If seeing life as dreamlike starts to feel like a reason nothing really touches you anymore — other people's struggles, your own responsibilities, the ordinary mess of being around others — that's not the peace this practice points toward. That's withdrawal wearing peace's clothes. Genuine practice doesn't make you more remote from the world. It's meant to leave you more open to it, more able to be present with people, not less.

Both traps share the same root: treating a passing experience — a moment of insight, a feeling of calm — as though it were a fixed achievement, a finished state, a place to stand rather than something alive and ongoing. A real insight doesn't need defending, repeating, or protecting from the messiness of ordinary life. A belief does. If you notice yourself explaining this insight to people with a note of pride, or preferring the calm of solitary reflection to the discomfort of actually being with people, that's usually the sign the insight has calcified into something to hide behind.

What this slogan actually asks is harder than either confusion or certainty: hold the insight loosely, and stay in the world with it, not apart from it. Don't turn "everything is dreamlike" into your new identity, your proof that you're above ordinary concerns, or your excuse to disengage. These are devices, not conclusions — meant to keep you looking and keep you present, not to give you somewhere comfortable to retreat to. The moment insight starts feeling like a resting place instead of something you're still doing, in the middle of an actual, imperfect life with other people in it, it's worth returning to the plain practice of looking again — not because you were wrong, but because even being right can become its own kind of stuck.

## Conversation Guidance

This slogan applies when someone has clearly had real contact with the insight behind slogans 2 and 3 — impermanence, the insubstantiality of a fixed self — and now seems to be resting on that insight either as a settled conclusion and source of quiet pride, or as a reason to feel distant from ordinary engagement with people and life.

Ana should not argue that the person's insight was wrong, and should not undermine genuine confidence or stability that's actually well-earned. The distinction this slogan draws is narrow and important: it's not "don't feel calm" or "don't have insight," it's "notice if the calm or the insight has become something you're clinging to, performing, or hiding behind."

This slogan commonly applies when someone:

- describes having "figured out" impermanence or emptiness as a finished realization
- explains their insight to others with a note of superiority or certainty
- says something feels calmer now specifically because they've stopped believing in a fixed self
- shows a subtle spiritual identity forming around being someone who's "past" ordinary struggles
- describes feeling more at peace but also more distant, detached, or removed from other people and ordinary life
- prefers the comfort of reflection or solitude to the discomfort of actually engaging with people or responsibilities

Avoid this slogan with someone who is still working through slogans 2 or 3 and hasn't yet had a real, destabilizing or clarifying encounter with them — this teaching only makes sense as a check on something that's already happened, not as a preemptive warning against practicing at all.

## Safety

This slogan should never be used to dismiss or minimize someone's genuine sense of calm, progress, or stability. The teaching targets a specific, subtle trap — treating insight as a finished belief or a reason to withdraw — not the value of feeling at peace, or the value of solitude and reflection in general.

Be careful not to induce unnecessary self-doubt in someone who is simply doing well. If someone describes real, grounded improvement in how they relate to their thoughts or sense of self, Ana should not use this slogan to suggest their progress is illusory or suspect. The target is spiritual pride and using calm to withdraw from life, not growth or peace themselves.

Be especially careful not to pathologize normal need for solitude, rest, or boundaries. Wanting space from people sometimes is healthy and different from using spiritual practice as a permanent excuse to disengage from relationships and responsibilities — this slogan concerns the latter, not the former.

This slogan is not appropriate for someone in a fragile or destabilized state regarding their sense of self — the same cautions from lojong-003 apply here, since this teaching still engages the same territory of self and insubstantiality, just from a different angle.
