## Memo Interpretation

There's a kind of hardening that can happen quietly, over time, usually as protection. Enough disappointment, enough people who weren't what they seemed, enough hard truths about how the world works, and it starts to feel safer — smarter, even — to expect less. To call this realism. To treat hope, delight, or openness as something naive, something only a child or a fool would still have room for.

This slogan pushes back on that directly. It doesn't ask you to ignore the harder side of things — the self-interest, the disappointment, the genuine harshness that shows up in the world and in people. That side is real, and pretending otherwise isn't what's being asked. What's being questioned is the belief that this harder view is the *only* accurate one — that seeing clearly means seeing only the difficult parts, and that anything softer or more hopeful must be a failure of clear sight rather than a different, equally real way of seeing.

Children haven't earned their way to some grand truth. But their world isn't less real than the guarded adult one, either — it's just built on a willingness the guarded world has usually given up: the willingness to marvel at something ordinary, to be delighted without needing to first confirm it's safe, to believe good things might actually happen. That willingness isn't something to outgrow. It can be recalled, practiced, returned to — not instead of clear-eyed awareness of hardship, but alongside it.

This slogan follows directly from resting in openness on the cushion — the same openness, carried now into an ordinary day, an ordinary conversation, an ordinary difficult person. Not by leaving your adult awareness behind, but by refusing to let the walls built out of old disappointment be the only thing standing between you and what's actually in front of you right now.

## Conversation Guidance

This slogan tends to land with someone who's noticed their own guardedness starting to calcify into identity — describing themselves as realistic, over it, or just not someone who gets their hopes up anymore, often in a tone that sounds resigned rather than genuinely at peace.

**Trap:** treating cynicism or guardedness as simply accurate perception — "I'm just being honest about how things are" — rather than recognizing it as one particular stance among others, adopted for protection and now mistaken for the whole truth.

**Shift:** naming that clear sight doesn't require choosing the harshest available interpretation — that openness and delight aren't blind spots, they're a different, equally real way of meeting what's in front of you.

**Practice:** something small and immediate — pausing on something ordinary right now (what's actually visible out a window, the specific texture of a moment) and looking at it with real curiosity instead of the usual pass-over. This can extend to a person who's been difficult: not excusing what they've done, but genuinely looking at them for a moment without the defended posture already loaded and ready.

Avoid turning this into "just choose to be positive" — that reads as denial to someone who has real reasons for their guardedness. The move is toward including wonder alongside clear awareness of difficulty, not replacing one with the other.

## Safety

Should not be used to encourage naivety about situations involving real risk — abusive relationships, financial exploitation, or people who have shown a genuine pattern of harm. This slogan concerns loosening a *general* posture of guardedness that has hardened into identity, not overriding legitimate, evidence-based caution about a specific person or situation.

Be careful with anyone describing guardedness that developed after trauma or repeated harm. For them, that guardedness may be a necessary, hard-won form of self-protection rather than an unnecessary wall — this slogan is not an invitation to drop protections that are actually serving someone's safety. If a user's guardedness is tied to a specific person or situation with a real pattern of harm, this is not the right reflection; the conversation should support their discernment rather than push toward openness.
